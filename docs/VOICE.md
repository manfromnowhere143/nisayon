# Voice

The voice is Daniel's: ambitious about the problem, exact about the evidence,
direct with the reader. Technical depth should make the work easier to inspect.

## Standing authorship rule

Daniel's instruction, 18 September 2026: for his work performed in these
sessions, preserve Daniel Wahnich as Git author and committer with his already
configured human email. Do not invent an address, alter global identity settings,
or attribute another person's work to him.

Do not credit Codex, Claude, Claude Code, Fable, an AI model, OpenAI or Anthropic
as an author, committer, co-author, contributor, maintainer or distributor of
Nisayon's own work. This applies to commit messages, PRs, release notes, READMEs,
contributor lists, package metadata and other public attribution. Do not add
model trailers, generated-by footers, assistant signatures or session credits.

Keep factual model identifiers, settings, tool names, experiment records,
provenance, citations and required third-party license attribution. This rule
does not erase model use or establish independent human review.

Write directly and precisely, with concrete mechanisms and measured results.
Commit subjects identify the actual change in the repository's existing style.
When a body helps, explain the triggering problem, resulting behavior, relevant
checks and material limits. Avoid generic progress messages, session transcripts,
praise, AI branding, invented credentials and affiliations.

Before committing, verify the effective author and committer and inspect the
actual message and trailers. Before publication, inspect outgoing project-authored
commits and public attribution surfaces. Report any historical violation with
its exact commit; do not rewrite shared, published or artifact-bound history,
or move tags to remove it.

Start with something concrete. A grasp fails after a deployment change. Two
explanations fit the trace. The next experiment should tell us which correction
to try. That is enough to earn a reader's attention.

## What to keep

- A clear position and a reason for it.
- Short explanations connected to actual mechanisms.
- Equations when they expose a distinction prose would hide.
- Results with denominators, comparator, cost and boundary.
- Failed experiments stated plainly, with the implication for the next step.
- Occasional warmth and wit. The reader is a capable colleague.

## What to remove

- Unsupported “frontier,” “revolutionary,” “unprecedented,” or “state of the art.”
- Generic startup sentences that could describe any company.
- Fake academic affiliations, credentials, customer relationships or endorsements.
- Repeated contrast slogans, theatrical certainty, and adjective chains.
- A history of every internal review in the README.
- Decorative certificates, badges or diagrams that imply an achieved capability.
- Administrative language where an ordinary verb explains the action.

Say: “The patch completes 18 of 20 declared tasks; the baseline completes 19.
We reject it under the frozen non-inferiority rule.” Use real numbers only when
retained results supply them; this sentence is an illustrative writing example.

Say: “The action changed. The later observations came from the old trajectory.
That replay cannot answer this question.” Then show the valid experiment.

The oath belongs in three sentences. The architecture belongs in mechanisms and
interfaces. The claim belongs next to the evidence that could overturn it.
